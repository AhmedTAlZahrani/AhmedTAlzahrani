# Ahmed Al-Zahrani

Riyadh-based. I like problems where the data is messy, the stakeholders
disagree, and the decision is too expensive to get wrong. Most of what I
work on sits somewhere in Saudi transport and infrastructure — crash
severity, ridership, logistics, energy grids. Some of it is machine
learning, some of it is just very careful counting.

The first thing I reach for on a new problem isn't a model, it's a
different framing. Most of the hard problems I've worked on got easier
once someone asked "what if we looked at this sideways?" Models are the
last 20% of the work.

## How I think about this work

- **I don't pick a model before I know who's going to read its output.**
  A linear model with readable coefficients is the right answer in
  regulated or policy contexts. Gradient boosting is the right answer
  when the goal is pure prediction. The choice is a conversation, not a
  default.

- **I assume a problem is harder than it looks until a baseline tells me
  otherwise.** If a majority-class classifier gets 94% on an imbalanced
  test set, a fancy model reporting 95% isn't the story — the baseline is.

- **Prediction isn't causation, and I try not to conflate them.** A lot
  of decisions get made on "the model predicts X" when the honest
  statement is "we can't disentangle X from three other things that move
  with it." I built a causal-inference study alongside the predictive
  work in this portfolio to keep myself honest about this.

## Tools I work with

Most of my work is in Python — scikit-learn, XGBoost and LightGBM for
tabular problems, PyTorch when the problem genuinely needs a neural
network, statsmodels and econml when I need proper statistical
inference, pandas and DuckDB for analytical work, GeoPandas for spatial
data. I use FastAPI for serving, Streamlit for quick dashboards, and
Docker when something needs to leave my machine. Comfortable with AWS
(SageMaker, Lambda, S3) but I'll reach for a boring SQL-and-a-cron
solution before a managed-service chain when it fits.
